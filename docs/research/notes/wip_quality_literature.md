# WIP / congestion vs. defect rates, and rebuilding WIP from timestamps: literature notes for OptiProductio

Scope: (a) empirical WIP/congestion–quality evidence, (b) mechanisms, (c) building WIP, flow time and throughput series from event logs, (d) observational designs for WIP→failure. Already-known anchors (Little 2011; Cordon 1995; Thürer et al. 2023; Gosavi & Gosavi 2024) are not re-summarised except where needed for context.

Verification legend: **[V]** = bibliographic details checked this session against a publisher, RePEc/IDEAS or indexer page. **[M]** = from background knowledge, not checked this session; confirm the DOI before citing.

## Which published studies quantify a WIP/congestion–quality relationship, and how big are the effects?

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed manufacturing study that directly regresses part-level defect probability on concurrent line WIP. The best quantified evidence comes from (i) firm- or plant-level JIT/inventory-reduction studies, where lower WIP goes with better productivity and quality and the effect shows up about a year later, and (ii) hospital "workload–quality" econometrics. In hospitals the effects are nonlinear, with tipping points near 92–93% occupancy. A Bosch-based WIP→failure test would therefore be fairly novel. Expect a small, possibly threshold-shaped effect, not a linear one.

### Cited Findings
**Manufacturing / operations (firm- or plant-level)**
- Lieberman & Demeester (1999), *Management Science* 45(4):466–485, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.45.4.466 **[V]**. Panel of 52 Japanese automotive firms. Each 10% inventory (WIP) reduction was associated with roughly a 1% gain in labour productivity, appearing about one year later. The effect was faster at Toyota affiliates and not detectable among close Nissan suppliers. Note that the outcome is productivity, not defects. The lag structure is useful for designing lagged-WIP tests. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v45y1999i4p466-485.html)
- Alles, Amershi, Datar & Sarkar (2000), "Information and Incentive Effects of Inventory in JIT Production", *Management Science* 46(12):1528–1544, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.46.12.1528.12079 **[V]**. A model in which low inventory helps workers observe and understand the process and so raises reliability and yield. The authors report that data from 116 plants worldwide support the link from reduced inventory to process reliability, product quality and cost. The abstract gives no effect size. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v46y2000i12p1528-1544.html); [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/581612ebcd505d5387558ec3b4d68991/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Semiconductor fabs, trade/practitioner source (not peer-reviewed). FabTime/INFICON argue that shorter cycle time "will tend to improve yields", through less contamination exposure, faster detection and more learning cycles. They also note reverse causation: lots on hold have both longer cycle times and worse yields. They give no quantification and no academic citations. — [INFICON/FabTime](https://www.inficon.com/en/news/fab-tradeoffs-higher-cycle-time-for-higher-yield)
- Wein, L. M. (1992), "On the relationship between yield and cycle time in semiconductor wafer fabrication", *IEEE Trans. Semiconductor Manufacturing* 5(2):156–158 **[M]**. This is the classic short analytical note arguing that cycle-time (queue-time) reduction raises yield via reduced contamination exposure. I could not retrieve a publisher page this session; verify via IEEE Xplore before citing.

**Healthcare / services analogues (workload–quality)**
- KC & Terwiesch (2009), *Management Science* 55(9):1486–1498, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1090.1037 **[V]**. Patient transport and cardiothoracic surgery. A 10% increase in load cut length of stay by about 2 days, and a 20% load increase cut transport time by about 30 s (workers speed up). Sustained overwork reverses this: a 1% increase in overwork adds about 6 h to length of stay, and a 10% increase in overwork is associated with about a 2% increase in mortality likelihood. Load-driven early discharge correlates with a small rise in mortality. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v55y2009i9p1486-1498.html)
- Kuntz, Mennicken & Scholtes (2015), "Stress on the Ward: Evidence of Safety Tipping Points in Hospitals", *Management Science* 61(4):754–771, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2014.1917 **[V]**. 82,280 patients, 256 departments, 83 German hospitals. Mortality tipping point at about 92.5% occupancy. 17% of patients experienced above-threshold occupancy in their first 7 days, and among them high occupancy accounted for about 1 in 7 deaths. Capacity pooling reduced occupancy-attributable deaths by 34%. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v61y2015i4p754-771.html); [JBS working paper PDF](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/120806-stress-on-the-ward.pdf)
- Berry Jaeker & Tucker (2017), "Past the Point of Speeding Up…", *Management Science* 63(4):1042–1062, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2015.2387 **[V for DOI/pages; volume/issue M]**. Two years of data from 203 California hospitals. The occupancy–LOS relation is N-shaped: LOS rises, then falls (speed-up), then rises again past a second tipping point at 93% occupancy (saturation). Flexible labour at high occupancy could have saved up to $138M over 23 months. — [SUFE abstract page](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/392907); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2387)
- Other analogues worth citing if space allows, all **[M]**, verify before citing: Tan & Netessine (2014), "When does the devil make work?", *Management Science*, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2013.1838, an inverted-U between workload and worker productivity in restaurants. Madsen, Ladelund & Linneberg (2014), *Health Affairs* 33(7), high bed occupancy associated with higher in-hospital and 30-day mortality in Denmark. Powell, Savin & Savva (2012), *M&SOM*, physician workload and documentation/reimbursement quality.

**Analytical models in the same space** (not empirical, but they show the expected shape)
- Agnihothri & Kenett (1995), "The impact of defects on a process with rework", *EJOR*. They link the defect distribution to yield, lead time and WIP in a process with 100% inspection and rework. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/818c8553127a5067b0a8bad79fc8c073/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Kang et al. (2017), *IJPR*, a WIP-based lot-size model with random reworkable and non-reworkable defect rates. It is cost-optimisation and offers no empirical test. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3b144a3c88485f3882ff454156d7204b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- An ERP/process-mining study at an Ecuadorian steel maker (Rodríguez-Flores et al., 2026, *Information*): across 3,740 orders and 31,791 events, rework, accumulated waiting and resource congestion were positively associated with deadline violation (a delivery outcome, not a defect outcome). This is a recent example of congestion features computed from event logs and fed into a logistic model. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/22acb447cd795cec89cd814ac9463a79/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- Healthcare evidence consistently shows nonlinearity: a "speed-up" region with flat or even improved metrics, then degradation past about 90%+ utilisation. For Bosch, test WIP as a nonlinear term (splines, WIP deciles, or threshold regression in the style of Kuntz et al.), not only a linear slope. A null linear result would not rule out a threshold effect.
- The manufacturing evidence is mostly firm- or plant-level and cross-temporal, at the scale of months to years. A within-line, minute-scale WIP→failure test on Bosch data is a different, more granular question. Frame it as exploratory and novel, not as a replication.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed manufacturing study giving a part-level odds ratio for defect vs. concurrent WIP. This should be stated as a gap, not filled with guesses.
- Wein (1992) and the healthcare items marked [M] were not verified this session.
- Alles et al.'s empirical effect size (116 plants) is not in the abstract; it needs the full text.

## What mechanisms link WIP to defects, and what are the main confounders? How do papers handle them?

### Takeaway
The main mechanisms are: detection delay, where more WIP between creation and inspection means more parts made under an undetected bad state; exposure and deterioration time in queue; worker overload and speed-up; and the masking of problems by buffers. The dominant threat to inference is reverse causality: failures and rework create WIP. Other confounders are product mix and routing, time trends and shift effects, and throughput or demand shocks. The workload papers handle these with fixed effects, instruments, lagged or exogenous load measures and threshold models.

### Cited Findings
**Mechanisms**
- Detection-delay amplification. When defects made at one station are detected only downstream and reworked, WIP between the stations can grow without bound for any failure rate under Markovian arrivals. This gives a rationale for JIT WIP limits (Cordon 1995, EJOR, already known to the caller). — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ce9857ee9ddf55eeaa01750c03119f11/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Information or "visibility". Low inventory lets workers observe and understand the process and improve its reliability and yields. — [Alles et al. 2000, IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v46y2000i12p1528-1544.html)
- Exposure time and contamination, which apply to fabs and to time-sensitive processes. Longer cycle time means more exposure and slower feedback. — [INFICON/FabTime](https://www.inficon.com/en/news/fab-tradeoffs-higher-cycle-time-for-higher-yield)
- Worker speed-up and saturation. Under load, service speeds up (possibly cutting corners), and past saturation both speed and quality deteriorate. — [KC & Terwiesch 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v55y2009i9p1486-1498.html); [Berry Jaeker & Tucker 2017](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/392907)
- Buffer depletion and escalation exhaustion as the cause of tipping points. — [Kuntz et al. 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v61y2015i4p754-771.html)
- Factory Physics (Hopp & Spearman, 3rd ed. 2008, Waveland; 2nd ed. 2000, McGraw-Hill) **[M]**. The quality chapter argues that low WIP shortens the time between defect creation and detection and so limits the number of defectives produced under an out-of-control state. It treats rework as an added "variability/flow" burden that inflates cycle time and WIP, so the causation runs both ways. The CONWIP chapter is the standard source for WIP caps.
- Porteus (1986), "Optimal lot sizing, process quality improvement and setup cost reduction", *Operations Research* 34(1):137–144, DOI 10.1287/opre.34.1.137 **[M]**. The classic model in which a process can go out of control during a lot, so the expected number of defectives rises with lot size (batch WIP). It is the analytical root of "smaller batches/WIP → fewer defects".

**Confounders and how papers handle them**
- Reverse causality (failure → rework/holds → more WIP and longer cycle time) is explicitly noted for fabs: held lots have both longer CT and worse yield. — [INFICON/FabTime](https://www.inficon.com/en/news/fab-tradeoffs-higher-cycle-time-for-higher-yield); modelled in [Cordon 1995](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ce9857ee9ddf55eeaa01750c03119f11/?utm_source=claude_desktop) and [Agnihothri & Kenett 1995](https://consensus.app/papers/details/818c8553127a5067b0a8bad79fc8c073/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- Endogeneity of load in hospital studies. KC & Terwiesch use econometric controls and model "overwork" (cumulative load) separately from instantaneous load. Kuntz et al. measure occupancy exposure in a fixed early window (first 7 days after admission) and estimate threshold (tipping-point) models across departments. Berry Jaeker & Tucker use patient-level panel data across 203 hospitals with nonlinear occupancy terms. — sources as above. (The detailed IV and fixed-effects specifications are in the full texts and were not read this session.)
- Lags. Lieberman & Demeester find the inventory→productivity effect with roughly a one-year lag and test lead/lag ordering to argue direction. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v45y1999i4p466-485.html)
- Heterogeneity by group (Toyota vs. Nissan affiliates) means effects need not be uniform across lines or products. — same source.

### Inferences (design recommendations for the Bosch test)
- **Exposure definition to limit reverse causality.** Measure WIP at the part's *entry* time (or averaged over its first k stations), not over its whole flow time. A failing part's own rework or retest inflates its flow time and the WIP it co-exists with. Also exclude the focal part from its own WIP count.
- **Lagged WIP.** Add WIP from a window before entry (e.g. the mean over the preceding 1–4 h) to emulate Lieberman-style lead/lag reasoning. As a placebo test, check whether *future* WIP (after the part exits) "predicts" failure. If it does, that points to reverse causality or common shocks.
- **Model.** Use a logistic regression of Response (0/1) on WIP (spline or deciles), with controls for route/product family (Bosch line and station path signature), time fixed effects (day or week, hour of day or shift proxy) and throughput rate. Use cluster-robust SEs by day. Alternatively, aggregate to hourly bins and run a Poisson or negative-binomial model of failures with log(parts exited) as an offset. Report odds ratios per +1 SD WIP.
- **Product-mix confounding is probably the biggest issue in Bosch.** Parts follow different station paths with different base failure rates, and mix shifts change both WIP and failure rate. Path or line fixed effects, or stratification by the dominant path, are essential.
- **Rare outcome.** About 0.58% of parts fail (≈6,900 failures among ~1.18M train parts; see below). Logistic regression is fine, but report absolute risk differences too, and use penalised or Firth logistic for small strata.
- **Backtesting the CONWIP alert.** Treat it as a classifier on time bins (alert when WIP > cap) and report precision/recall for "high-failure-rate hour". Choose the cap on an earlier time block and evaluate on a later one, never on the same period. Respect time ordering in any split.

### Gaps
- I found no published study that applies these designs to the Bosch dataset's timestamps for a WIP→quality question. The Bosch literature found is almost entirely ML failure prediction.
- Factory Physics chapter and page numbers were not checked.

## What is the standard way to build WIP, flow time and throughput series from entry/exit timestamps, and what are the pitfalls?

### Takeaway
The standard approach is the sample-path one. Take per-part entry time a_i and exit time d_i. Then WIP is L(t) = #{i : a_i ≤ t < d_i}, built by sorting +1/−1 events and taking a cumulative sum. Flow time is W_i = d_i − a_i, and throughput is the number of exits per bin. Check consistency with finite-window Little's law, L̄ ≈ λ̄·W̄. The main pitfalls are edge or censoring effects (parts already in the system at the window start, or still in it at the end), non-stationarity, and, for Bosch specifically, that the timestamps are measurement times, not true entry and exit times.

### Cited Findings
- Kim & Whitt (2013), "Statistical Analysis with Little's Law", *Operations Research* 61(4):1030–1045, DOI 10.1287/opre.2013.1193 **[V]**. Real data cover finite intervals that are neither long-run averages nor stationary. The authors give confidence intervals via batch means on a single sample path, and show how to estimate and remove the bias caused by edge effects when the system does not start and end empty. Illustrated with call-centre data. This is the go-to citation for "Little's law on observed data". — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/oropre/v61y2013i4p1030-1045.html); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2013.1193)
- Related: Glynn & Whitt (1989), "Indirect estimation via L = λW", *Operations Research* **[M for details]** — [Stanford page](https://web.stanford.edu/~glynn/papers/1989/GW89b.html). A time-varying Little's law for estimating waiting times appears in *Probability in the Engineering and Informational Sciences* — [Cambridge](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/probability-in-the-engineering-and-informational-sciences/article/estimating-waiting-times-with-the-time-varying-littles-law/18BF94EF65D06A5E52B72D1F81F3BFE0) (authors and year not checked this session).
- Minimal-information reconstruction at Bosch. Brochado et al. (2022), *Int. J. Management Science and Engineering Management*, show that the timestamp at which each product exits each station is enough to build an abstract line layout (a "queue directed graph") and to compute production metrics and detect bottlenecks (Active Period method). This is validated on a Bosch Thermotechnology plant in Portugal. It is a different plant from the Kaggle data, but very close to the OptiProductio use case. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/19e0c24a112e5763ada25034bee15b2a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Process mining in manufacturing. Event logs (case id, activity, timestamp) support discovery, conformance checking and performance analysis. Replaying the log on a model links timestamps to model elements and exposes bottlenecks (van der Aalst et al. 2012, *WIREs DMKD*). — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6930779234d85dd7b93bb0746895071c/?utm_source=claude_desktop). Manufacturing applications: Lorenz et al. (2021, *IJPR*), productivity improvement in make-to-stock manufacturing at a sanitary-products maker — [Consensus](https://consensus.app/papers/details/db7d4a7c8b3b5492b1baaa940f5fac63/?utm_source=claude_desktop). Knoll et al. (2019, *Expert Syst. Appl.*), multidimensional process mining for value-stream mapping of 7,500 parts at a German automotive OEM — [Consensus](https://consensus.app/papers/details/45acf3d734bb5cfda25dcaf4e5b82a33/?utm_source=claude_desktop). Choueiri et al. (2020, *J. Manufacturing Systems*), remaining cycle-time prediction from manufacturing logs — [Consensus](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d2012203560659648be7e21eef52435f/?utm_source=claude_desktop). - Bosch Kaggle dataset facts: about 1,183,165 parts, 4,264 station features, 0.58% labelled failures — [Zdravevski et al. 2026, Sci. Rep.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/467917f480245d3dbb8781e199ef1386/?utm_source=claude_desktop). ### Inferences (practical recipe and pitfalls; my synthesis, not from a single source)
1. **Entry and exit proxies.** In `train_date.csv`, each column is the time a feature was *measured* at a station. Use entry ≈ min(non-null timestamps) and exit ≈ max(non-null timestamps) per part, and per station for station-level WIP. Flow time is then a lower bound (it excludes time before the first and after the last measurement). Units are anonymised. Kaggle community analysis suggests 1 unit ≈ 6 minutes (0.01 week), but this is **unverified, community-derived**, so state it as an assumption.
2. **Include test-set parts in WIP.** The Kaggle train and test parts are interleaved in time. Building WIP from train parts only would undercount concurrent parts by about half. Use `train_date` + `test_date` to build L(t), and labels from train only. This comes from community knowledge **[M]**; confirm by checking that the time ranges overlap.
3. **Event-sweep algorithm.** Create events (a_i, +1) and (d_i, −1), sort them (process exits before entries at ties, or document the convention), take the cumulative sum to get L(t), then resample to a time-weighted average per bin. Throughput is exits per bin. Cross-check that mean L ≈ (exits per unit time) × mean W within each window, as in Kim & Whitt.
4. **Edge and censoring effects.** Parts that enter before the window or leave after it bias WIP, and especially mean W, downward near the boundaries. Drop a warm-up and cool-down margin of at least about the 95th-percentile flow time, or use the Kim & Whitt edge-bias correction. Watch for data gaps (shutdowns, weekends) that look like zero WIP.
5. **Outliers.** Very long flow times (holds or retests) dominate mean W. Report medians and trimmed means, and remember these long-flow parts are disproportionately failures, which is the reverse-causality path again.
6. **Per-part exposure variable.** For each part i, use L(a_i) − 1, or the mean WIP over the hour before a_i, as the regressor. That gives one row per part for logistic regression.

### Gaps
- No published work was found that reconstructs WIP time series specifically from the Kaggle Bosch `*_date.csv` files. The time-unit and train/test-interleaving claims need checking by the student directly against the data.
- Authors and year of the PEIS "time-varying Little's law" paper were not captured.
